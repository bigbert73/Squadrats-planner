Squadrats Route Planner

Every ride claims ground.

How the game works

What squares are

Squadrats divides the world map into a fixed grid. Every square you ride through becomes yours. The planner knows this grid to the metre and counts what you will collect before you leave home.

  • Squadrat (SQ)A map tile at zoom 14, about 1.5 km across. The big scale: new areas.
  • Squadratinho (SQI)A tile at zoom 17, about 191 m across. One squadrat holds 64 of them. Every lane counts here.
  • Yard / YardinhoCollected squares whose four neighbours are all collected too. They measure how solid your territory is.
  • Übersquadrat / ÜbersquadratinhoThe largest solid N×N square without a single gap, e.g. 25 × 25. The hardest score to grow.
Quick start

Your first route in four steps

From sign-up to a route on your bike computer takes a few minutes. After that it is a single tap, because the planner remembers your home and settings.

  1. Create an accountOne click on Continue with Strava creates the account and fetches your rides straight away. A username and password or a Google account work too.
  2. Load your ridesIf you came in with Strava, they are already there. With a regular account, connect Strava in your profile or import the Squadrats.com KML or GPX files.
  3. Set your homeType your address in the profile or pick it on the map. Every route will start there.
  4. Plan and ridePick a distance, press “Calculate route”, download the GPX for your Garmin.
Route planning

Loops that collect squares and never ride back the same road

You give a start and a distance. The planner tries dozens of loop variants on real roads, counts the new squares of each and keeps the one that collects most with as little repeated road as possible.

  • Loop or A → BA loop from home over a set distance (5–200 km), or a point-to-point ride with an optional return by a different road.
  • Results at a glanceRoute card: distance, time at your average speed, new SQ and SQI, climbing, and the share of road ridden twice.
  • Full controlDrag points, click the route to add a via point, undo, reverse the direction, save favourite routes.
  • All of EuropePlan routes and loops from Portugal to Finland, on the planner’s own routing engine. Places along the route and squares without a road are ready for Poland, Germany, the Netherlands, Belgium and France. When a point is outside the coverage or on water, the planner says so plainly.

On a computer

  1. Press Start on the bar above the map and click the map, or type an address in “From?”.
  2. Choose Loop or A → B and set the distance with the slider.
  3. Choose a bike profile and, under Advanced, what to optimise for.
  4. Press Calculate route (or hit Enter).
  5. Drag any point, or click the route line to add a via point. Only the changed leg is recalculated.

On a phone

  1. Tap Plan route on the bottom bar. With a home set, the start is already filled in.
  2. Pick loop or A → B, the distance and a bike profile.
  3. Tap Calculate route. The sheet folds down to the result card.
  4. Press and drag a point on the map to adjust the course.
  5. Swipe the sheet up for the summary, points and saved routes.

On foot and running

Runners and walkers collect squares too. Below the bike profiles there are two more: On foot and Run. The route then follows trails, paths and pavements, including places closed to bikes. The distance slider switches to 3–60 km and the time is counted from a walking (5 km/h) or running (10 km/h) pace, which you can change on the route card. Both profiles use the same network of paths; they differ in pace and in the activity type written to the GPX file.

Weather and wind on the route

Every route card shows the forecast for the time of the ride: temperature, rain, mean wind and a bar splitting the route into headwind, crosswind and tailwind. The planner works this out for successive places on the route at the hour you will get there. The list next to the temperature sets the start time, up to two days ahead. The forecast comes from MET Norway.

For a loop planned from just a start point, tick Loop into the wind first. The planner then picks a loop on the side of the start the wind blows from: you ride out into the wind and come home with it at your back. Reversing a loop does not change this; what matters is on which side of home the loop lies. The option works from 11 km/h of wind and may give slightly fewer new squares, because it narrows the choice.

Result and elevation profile

The route card shows the key numbers, with a surface summary below: kilometres of tarmac, cycle paths, gravel and cobbles. The elevation profile opens under the map. Slide a finger or the mouse along the chart and the point moves along the route. The route itself carries kilometre markers whose spacing adapts to the map zoom.

Drawing a route point by point

When you know where you want to ride, draw the route yourself, like in Strava’s route builder. Tap the start, then each next point, and every leg is routed on roads straight away with your bike profile. Kilometres, new squares and the elevation profile grow as you go. A dashed line marks a leg still being routed. Keep tapping, the next legs queue up.

On a computer

  1. Click Draw on the bar above the map (or press D).
  2. Click the start, then the next points. The route grows leg by leg.
  3. Undo (Ctrl+Z) removes the last point and Redo (Ctrl+Y) restores what you undid by accident. Adding a new point clears the redo list.
  4. Drag a point somewhere else: only the two legs next to it are re-routed, the rest of the route stays as it was.
  5. Click a point, or the pencil next to it in the waypoint list, to give it a short name (up to 20 characters). The name shows under the point and goes into the GPX file as a waypoint for your bike computer. The list also shows which kilometre of the route each point is at.
  6. Back to start closes the loop, on other roads than the way out where possible.
  7. Done (or Enter / Esc) finishes: the route gets the full card, surfaces, stops and photos, GPX and saving.

On a phone

  1. Tap Draw on the bar at the top of the map.
  2. Tap the start with your finger, then the next points. The running distance shows above the map.
  3. The icons by the distance: the left arrow undoes the last point, the right arrow restores it, the circle heads back to the start.
  4. Drag a pin with your finger to move the point. Only the two legs next to it are re-routed.
  5. Tap a pin, or the pencil in the waypoint list, to give the point a short name (up to 20 characters). The name shows under the pin and goes into the GPX file for Garmin. Next to each point the list shows its kilometre of the route.
  6. A double tap zooms in without adding a point. Pan the map with your finger as usual.
  7. Tap Done. To extend the route later, go back into Draw.
Smart collecting

Priorities: which squares to collect first

Not every missing square is worth the same. The planner scores every uncollected squadratinho within 30 km of home: how much it grows your Übersquadratinho and Yardinho, whether a road leads there and how far it is.

  • Three tiersThe map colours squares by value. The most valuable glow the brightest.
  • Ready-made goalsThe list says it plainly: “1 square missing for Über 27 × 27”, and how many kilometres from home.
  • A route in one clickPriorities mode builds a loop that collects the most points within your distance limit. Squares without a road are taken “with a short walk”.

On a computer

  1. The Priorities layer is on by default. You can turn it off in the Squares tab.
  2. Click a goal in the list and the map flies to that window.
  3. In Route → Advanced, choose Priorities, set the distance limit and calculate.
  4. After the ride syncs, the ranking recalculates itself, so the next loop aims at the next squares.

On a phone

  1. The Priorities layer is on by default. You can turn it off in the sheet, on the Squares tab.
  2. Tap a goal in the list to see it on the map.
  3. In Route, choose Priorities mode and tap Calculate route.
  4. Squares “with a short walk” get their own markers on the map.
Your territory

Squares, Yard and Über in one place

The Squares tab is your scoreboard: counters at both levels, map layers to switch on and tools to mark ground you can’t get into.

  • Twelve layersCollected, new from the route, Yard, Über, z14 and z17 grids, Priorities and “no road”. Each one switch, and your choice is remembered on your account.
  • UncollectableThree quick taps mark a square as unreachable (water, fenced land). The planner skips it and Über is counted around it.
  • “No road” hintsCrossed-out squares are places with no road or path in OpenStreetMap. You can accept them in bulk for the current view.

On a computer

  1. Open the Squares tab. The counters are always in the header too.
  2. Switch on layers, e.g. Yard and Übersquadrat.
  3. In View mode at zoom 14+, click a square three times to mark it uncollectable. Three more clicks undo it.
  4. Mark suggested in view accepts every “no road” hint on screen.

On a phone

  1. SQ, Yard, Über and SQI counters sit in the top bar.
  2. Open Squares in the sheet to switch layers.
  3. Tap a square three times quickly to mark it uncollectable. Works on iPhone and Android.
Know your route

See the route before you ride it

Once a route is calculated, the planner gathers what lies along it: where to eat, where to refill water, where to sleep, and what the road looks like in people’s photos and in Street View.

Points along the route

Six categories: food, water, shops (and Orlen fuel stations), lodging, bike service, sights. Every point shows its kilometre on the route and its distance off it, in riding order. The Only by the route filter keeps places within 60 m, so no detour is needed. A point’s popup has a Google Maps button with reviews and photos.

People’s photos along the route

Blue dots are Wikimedia Commons photos; green dots are Mapillary street-level images, often taken from a bike, sometimes 360° panoramas. Photos within 300 m of the route are shown, about one per 150 m. You can tell tarmac from gravel or sand before you get there. Photos fetched for an area are kept, so later routes there load instantly.

Places of interest from Wikipedia

Up to 15 places near the route that have a Wikipedia article: monuments, reserves, viewpoints. On the map they show a photo thumbnail; the popup has a short summary and a link to the article.

Street View

The Street View panel opens beside the map. Click any spot and the Google panorama shows what it looks like. An arrow on the map shows where you stand and which way you are looking.

On a computer

  1. After calculating, scroll the panel to Points along the route and filter by category.
  2. Click an item to zoom the map to that place.
  3. Click a camera dot to see the photo. Clicking the photo opens it full size.
  4. Map menu button (right side) → Street View, then click the map.

On a phone

  1. Map menu (sliders icon) → Points along the route shows the category filters.
  2. Tap a pin, then Google Maps — reviews, photos.
  3. The same menu toggles Route photos, Places of interest (Wikipedia) and split-screen Street View.

On the bike

From the planner to your bike computer

The route goes where you need it: to a Garmin, a Wahoo, or straight to your phone with your position on the route.

  • GPX for GarminA file with elevations, ready to import as a course in Garmin Connect.
  • SendThe system share sheet, e.g. to the Wahoo ELEMNT app.
  • RidingYour GPS position on the route: kilometre ridden, distance left, a warning when you leave the route.
  • My routesSaved routes with a preview, distance and climb. From a phone you send them to Garmin Connect in one tap.

A data field for Garmin Edge

Squadrats has its own Connect IQ data field for Edge 1040 and 1050 bike computers. While you ride it shows the square grid around you, collected and missing squadratinhos, and a counter of new squares from this ride. It fetches your area through the phone from your account using a personal key.

Illustration. Shown is a sketch of the data field screen, not a capture from the device.

My routes and sending to Garmin Connect

Any route, planned or drawn, can be kept with Save in its card. The My routes button in the header opens a list of all saved routes. Each shows an outline (the green dot is the start, the red one the finish of an A → B ride), distance, climb, new squares, date and bike profile. Search it by name and sort by newest, longest, shortest or most SQI.

On a phone, Garmin Connect opens the system share sheet with the route’s GPX file. On an iPhone the Connect app isn’t listed there, so save the file to the Files app, open it there, tap share and pick Connect: the route is saved as a course under its own name and reaches your device at the next sync. On a computer the same button downloads the file and opens the Garmin Connect courses page, where you click Import.

No Connect in the share sheet? Scroll the app row to the end and tap More. If it is not there either, import the downloaded file at connect.garmin.com → Courses → Import.

On a computer

  1. Click My routes in the header or All routes under the list in the Route tab.
  2. The outline, the name or On map loads the route with its full card. The pencil renames it, the bin deletes it (a second click confirms).
  3. Garmin Connect downloads the GPX and opens Garmin Connect courses: click Import and pick the file.

On a phone

  1. Tap the route icon in the header, next to your account.
  2. Open the planner at its https address (over http a phone cannot hand the file to an app). On a route, tap Garmin Connect, choose Save to Files, open the file in the Files app, tap share and pick Connect.
  3. In Garmin Connect choose the course type (e.g. road cycling or gravel) and save. The course syncs to your device. Point names appear only after you save and reopen the course; before saving they are blank.

A link to a route and other people’s GPX

In My routes the Link button creates a public address for a route. Whoever gets it sees the route on a map with its length and climb, can download the GPX or open it in their own planner, without creating an account. They do not see your squares, other routes or account name. By default the link hides the first and last 300 m, so it does not give away where you live. One click switches the link off, and from then on nobody can open it.

The other way round there is From a GPX file above the route list: it loads a friend’s route or one from another app. The planner shows its new squares, elevation profile and weather, and once saved it joins your routes.

Spoken turn announcements

In Riding mode the planner announces turns by voice: first ahead of time (“In 200 metres, turn left”), then at the turn itself. It also tells you when you leave the route, when you are back on it and when you are reaching the finish. The ride bar shows an arrow for the next turn with its distance, and the speaker icon switches the voice on and off. Announcements use the app’s language. A phone speaks only with the screen on and the planner open, so for a long ride a course sent to your bike computer can be handier.

An app on your home screen, and riding without coverage

You can install the planner like an app: choose Install the app in the account menu (on an iPhone: in Safari Share → Add to Home Screen). It then opens full screen, with its own icon. It needs an https address.

Without coverage the app opens from a copy kept on the phone and shows your account, squares, saved routes and the parts of the map you looked at before. An Offline marker appears at the top. Riding mode and spoken turns work on a loaded route. Planning a new route needs a connection. The map cannot be downloaded ahead, because map servers forbid it: before you leave, look over the area of your route at the zoom you will use.

On a computer

  1. On the route card, press Download GPX for Garmin; in Garmin Connect choose Courses → Import.
  2. Save adds the route to My routes (the button in the header, next to your account).
  3. GPX downloads a plain file for other apps.

On a phone

  1. Send opens sharing: pick Wahoo, Garmin Connect or Files.
  2. The navigation button on the right starts Riding mode. The top bar shows your kilometre and the distance left.
  3. Center returns to your position, Stop ends tracking.
Your rides

Sync and import

Squares come from your rides. The planner stores them once in its own database and then only fetches new activities.

  • Strava, automaticallyYou connect in one click, with no API keys and no setup. After that new rides arrive by themselves every 30 minutes. “Sync” does it right away.
  • KML from Squadrats.comThe official squares export from the Squadrats app loads in one file, including history from before Strava.
  • GPX filesDrag files onto the map or pick several at once. Works with any device.
  • StatsHuntersIt counts the same squares. Create a share link in StatsHunters, paste it in the Squares tab under Import from StatsHunters, and the planner fetches your squares. The link only lets it read your tiles.
  • VeloViewerA KML file with Explorer tiles exported from VeloViewer loads with Import from VeloViewer. Both imports add the big squares (SQ); the small ones (SQI) come from Strava, GPX or the Squadrats KML.
  • Disconnect any timeDisconnect Strava in the profile withdraws the app’s access at Strava and stops the sync. The squares you collected stay on your account.
  • Your own API keys (advanced)If you want a request limit of your own, paste the Client ID and Secret of your own Strava application in the profile. Regular use does not need them.

On a computer

  1. New here: Continue with Strava on the sign-in screen. Already have an account: Profile and Strava (account menu) → Connect with Strava. Approve the access on Strava’s page and leave the activities ticked.
  2. The first download starts by itself and the map moves to your rides. Later: Squares tab → Sync with Strava.
  3. Import GPX or Import from Squadrats.com (KML) in the same tab. GPX files can also be dropped on the map. The tab works without Strava too.

On a phone

  1. Tap Continue with Strava on the sign-in screen, or connect it once in your profile.
  2. After that there is nothing to do: the 30-minute sync runs on the server.
  3. GPX and KML import works from files on the phone (Files, iCloud, Drive).
Account and privacy

Your data under your control

Every user has their own account, their own Strava connection and their own squares. Nobody sees another person’s routes, squares or settings, even on a shared computer.

  • Sign-inStrava in one click, a username and password (8+ characters with a digit), a Google account or Apple (when the installation has them set up). If you came in with Strava, Google or Apple, you can set a backup password in the profile. Sessions last 30 days, and changing the password signs out your other devices.
  • Session ran out mid-ride planningA sign-in window appears over the map. The route and points stay on screen, so after signing in you simply carry on and save.
  • Several people, one deviceSigning out or switching accounts reloads the app: the previous person’s route, points and squares leave the screen.
  • Home pointAn address or a point picked on the map. Every new route starts from home.
  • Account deletionOne button deletes activities, squares, routes and keys, and disconnects the app from Strava.
  • Admin panelThe administrator manages user accounts.
  • Six languagesPolish, English, German, Dutch, French and Czech. The language list is in the account menu and on the sign-in screen. The change applies at once, without losing your route, and is remembered; spoken turns and the password e-mail use the chosen language too. A new visitor gets their browser’s language. The four new translations are machine-made; this handbook is in Polish and English.
  • Forgotten passwordOn the sign-in screen click Forgot your password? and give the e-mail address of your account. You get a link valid for an hour that works once; the new password signs out your other devices. You add or change the address in the profile (it asks for your current password). The option shows when the installation has outgoing mail set up.

Under the hood

Architecture: containers, databases and connections

Squadrats is three Docker containers and a few SQLite files. No cloud and no external database: it all runs on one Linux computer, with OpenShift as the planned production target. This chapter is for people who run, develop or review the app.

How data flows

Planning a loop

  1. The browser sends the start, length, profile and goal to /api/route/detour.
  2. The planner draws dozens of candidates: ellipses in 16 directions and “petals” towards clusters of uncollected squares.
  3. BRouter routes the best ones in two halves. The second avoids the first one’s roads, so the way back differs.
  4. The winner is the route that actually collects the most, keeps the length and doesn’t repeat roads.

Strava sync

  1. Strava OAuth sign-in stores tokens in squadrats.db. Behind a connection stands this installation’s shared Strava application (STRAVA_CLIENT_ID) or the person’s own keys, and each token remembers which one issued it.
  2. Every 5 minutes the backend checks whose sync is older than 30 minutes. On the shared application the interval grows with the number of riders, to stay within Strava’s daily limit (STRAVA_DAILY_BUDGET, 600 requests by default).
  3. After a 429 that application rests until the limit window is over. Other applications and sign-in keep working.
  4. New activities become SQ and SQI, and Yard, Über and priorities are recalculated.
  5. A manual sync shows live progress over SSE.

Photos along the route

  1. The backend returns photos from photos.db for areas it already knows.
  2. The browser fetches missing areas from Commons and posts them back, so the next person gets them at once.
  3. The backend reads Mapillary from vector tiles, and the thumbnail only on click.

Garmin data field

  1. The profile generates a personal Garmin key.
  2. Every 5 minutes the Edge sends its position and key to /api/garmin/area through the phone.
  3. The backend returns the square map around the rider, and the Edge draws it while riding.

Security and privacy

  • SessionsA JWT in an httpOnly cookie (SameSite=Lax) valid for 30 days. Page scripts cannot read it. Changing the password or deleting the account invalidates earlier sessions. The signing key is JWT_SECRET or, when unset, a random key in data/.jwt-secret.
  • RequestsWrites coming from another site are refused (403), every input is validated (bad data gives a 4xx, never a crash), and responses carry nosniff, frame blocking and a referrer policy.
  • Passwordsbcrypt hashes, at least 8 characters with a digit. Login: 10 tries per 15 minutes, sign-up: 5 per hour.
  • Service keysGoogle, Mapy.com, Mapillary and Strava only in the server’s .env. Mapy.com tiles and Mapillary photos go through a proxy, so those keys never reach the browser. The exception: a signed-in person receives the Google Maps key, because the Street View window needs it. Restrict it in Google Cloud to your app’s address.
  • LogsContainer logs are capped at 3 × 10 MB, as they contain IP addresses and positions.
